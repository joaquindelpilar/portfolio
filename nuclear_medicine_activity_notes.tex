\subsection{The physical chain}
\source{L1, approximately 11:00--31:00 and 62:00--73:00.}
Nuclear medicine uses radionuclides to investigate physiology and disease, and in selected applications to deliver therapy. A radiopharmaceutical combines a radionuclide with a chemical form that determines how it distributes in the body. Understanding the radionuclide alone is insufficient: chemical targeting, uptake and clearance determine where its radiation originates.

The medical physicist connects these stages: measuring activity, characterizing instruments, assessing quantitative accuracy, investigating artifacts and optimizing examinations. Physicians provide clinical interpretation and medical decisions; technologists perform procedures and assigned quality-control tasks; engineers service equipment. Responsibilities overlap and depend on local training and governance.

The orientation named six broad areas: nuclear foundations; radiation interactions; planar and gamma camera imaging; SPECT; PET and hybrid imaging; quantitative imaging, QA/QC, protection and theranostics. \section{Atoms, nuclei and notation}
\source{L1, 73:00--78:00; L2, 37:00--71:00.}
\subsection{Atomic models and electronic structure}
Dalton's atomic model, Thomson's electron-containing model, Rutherford's compact nucleus and Bohr's quantized orbits form a historical sequence. Modern quantum mechanics describes electrons using orbitals and probability distributions. Bohr's model is useful for hydrogen-like systems, but literal circular orbits do not describe general many-electron atoms.

The nucleus contains protons and neutrons, jointly called nucleons. It contains almost all atomic mass; electrons occupy the much larger surrounding region. A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons. Removing an electron creates a positive ion; adding one can create a negative ion. Ionization changes electronic charge without necessarily changing nuclear composition.

The Pauli principle permits at most two electrons with opposite spin projections in a given spatial orbital. In many-electron atoms, energy depends on more than $n$ alone because of screening and electron interactions.

\subsection{Nuclide notation and nuclear families}
Write a nuclide as $\nucl{A}{Z}{X}$, where
\[
 A=Z+N_n,\qquad Z=\text{proton number},\qquad N_n=\text{neutron number}.
\]
Here $N_n$ denotes neutrons \emph{within one nucleus}; later, $N(t)$ counts undecayed nuclei \emph{in a sample}. Likewise, $A$ in a nuclide label is mass number; $A(t)$ in a decay equation is activity. Mass number counts nucleons and is dimensionless; it is not the measured atomic mass in u.

\toprule Family & Same quantity & Example / distinction\\\midrule
Isotopes & $Z$ & $^1$H, $^2$H, $^3$H; different $N_n$.\\
Isotones & $N_n$ & $^2$H and $^3$He each have one neutron.\\
Isobars & $A$ & $^3$H and $^3$He each have three nucleons.\\
Nuclear isomers & $A,Z$ & $^{99m}$Tc and $^{99}$Tc; different nuclear energy states.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Hydrogen-1, hydrogen-2 and hydrogen-3 are protium, deuterium and tritium. Their nuclei are the proton, deuteron and triton. Hydronium ($\mathrm{H_3O^+}$) is a molecular ion, not another name for hydrogen-1.

An asterisk indicates an excited nuclear state. The suffix $m$ indicates a metastable nuclear state, which persists long enough to be identified separately. An ionic charge is placed at upper right, for example $\nucl{23}{11}{Na}^{+}$. Isotopes have closely similar electronic chemistry, although isotope effects occur; their nuclear decay properties can be very different.

\subsection{Size and radiation terminology}
A useful nuclear-radius estimate is
\[
R\simeq r_0 A^{1/3},\qquad r_0\simeq1.2\text{--}1.3~\mathrm{fm},\quad1~\mathrm{fm}=10^{-15}~\mathrm m.
\]
The lecture used 1.3 fm. The exponent acts on \emph{mass number}, not atomic number. This is an approximate nuclear-size model, particularly crude for very light nuclei.

Ionizing radiation can remove bound electrons through its interactions. Alpha and beta particles are directly ionizing; photons and neutrons transfer energy to secondary charged particles. Excitation raises an atom or nucleus to a higher state without necessarily ejecting a particle. Electronic excitation and nuclear excitation involve different structures and typically different energy scales.

\section{Fundamental interactions and nuclear stability}
\source{L1, 78:00--85:00; L2, 7:00--37:00 and 71:00--85:00; L4, 58:00--63:00.}
\subsection{Particles and forces: corrected framework}
Electrons are elementary leptons. Protons and neutrons are composite baryons with valence-quark contents $uud$ and $udd$. Up and down quark charges are $+2e/3$ and $-e/3$, respectively:
\[
Q_p=2(2e/3)-e/3=e,\qquad Q_n=2e/3-2e/3=0.
\]
These fractions describe \emph{charge}, not spin. Quarks have spin $1/2$. Baryons have half-integer spin, while mesons have integer spin; a baryon spin of $2/3$ is not the correct classification.

\begin{longtable}{P{0.17\linewidth}P{0.22\linewidth}P{0.49\linewidth}}
\toprule Interaction & Carrier / description & Relevance here\\\midrule
Strong & Gluons at quark level & Binds quarks; its residual nuclear interaction binds nucleons over nuclear distances.\\
Electromagnetic & Photon & Atomic binding, charged-particle interactions, nuclear photon transitions and proton repulsion.\\
Weak & $W^\pm,Z^0$ & Beta transformations and electron capture.\\
Gravitational & General relativity; graviton hypothetical & Negligible for ordinary nuclear calculations; not included in the Standard Model.\\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}
\clarify{Strong and weak do not mean ``stable-nucleus force'' and ``unstable-nucleus force.'' Both stable and radioactive nuclei have strong interactions. The type of transition determines the interaction involved. The four forces do not have equal intrinsic strengths. Dominance also depends on distance, energy and the system: gravity dominates many astronomical systems, while electromagnetic forces underlie chemistry and everyday contact forces.}
The Higgs boson is a scalar particle associated with the Higgs field, not the common gauge carrier of all interactions. Its 2012 discovery supported the Higgs mechanism; it was not the first validation of general relativity. The Standard Model contains strong and electroweak physics without constituting a demonstrated grand-unified theory. Supersymmetry, grand unification and string-theory proposals are distinct ideas; they should not be treated as synonymous established results. See the CERN references in Section~\ref{sec:refs}.

\subsection{Stability is an energy and structure question}
The competition between nuclear attraction, proton Coulomb repulsion, neutron--proton balance, shell structure and pairing affects stability. For many light stable nuclides $N_n\approx Z$; heavier stable nuclides generally have more neutrons than protons. Neutrons contribute nuclear binding without additional proton--proton repulsion. This trend does not mean arbitrarily adding neutrons always stabilizes a nucleus.

Pairing favors even numbers of protons and neutrons. The familiar light stable odd--odd nuclides are $^2$H, $^6$Li, $^{10}$B and $^{14}$N. Avoid memorizing a universal count of stable nuclides from one chart: classifications depend on what decay has been observed, long-lived exceptions and whether isomers are counted.

A long half-life does not make a radioactive nuclide strictly stable. A daughter can also remain radioactive. A decay is energetically possible when total final rest energy is lower, but barriers and selection rules strongly affect the rate. Increasing binding energy per nucleon is a useful trend, not a universal test for every decay channel; use the full mass balance.

\subsection{Reading a nuclide chart without reversing directions}
First read the axes. For a chart with horizontal $N_n$ and vertical $Z$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule Process & $\Delta N_n$ & $\Delta Z$ & Direction\\\midrule
$\alpha$ & $-2$ & $-2$ & Left and down\\
$\beta^-$ & $-1$ & $+1$ & Left and up\\
$\beta^+$ or EC & $+1$ & $-1$ & Right and down\\
Gamma / internal conversion & 0 & 0 & Same nuclide position\\
Neutron emission & $-1$ & 0 & Left\\
Proton emission & 0 & $-1$ & Down\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
If the axes are interchanged, the visual directions change while the numerical changes remain the same. Simultaneous increases in $N_n$ and $Z$ do not describe one of the listed ordinary spontaneous decays; nuclear capture or fusion reactions must be distinguished from decay. The IAEA LiveChart is useful for checking a particular nuclide's actual branches.

\subsection{Coulomb barriers and neutron interactions}
A positive projectile encounters repulsion approaching a positive nucleus; quantum tunneling can permit penetration below a classical barrier. Neutrons have no electrostatic Coulomb barrier, but their interactions still depend on energy and nuclear structure. Nuclear forces do not switch off at a perfectly sharp universal surface, and electromagnetic interactions do not disappear inside the nucleus.

Neutron hazard cannot be inferred from mass alone. Neutron energy, fluence and interaction pathways matter, including recoil protons and capture radiation. Ordinary alpha emission is not automatically accompanied by neutron emission. A source such as an alpha emitter mixed with a suitable target can generate neutrons through a separate $(\alpha,n)$ reaction.

\section{Mass, energy and the binding-energy exercises}
\source{L2, approximately 50:00--63:00 and 85:00--93:00.}
\subsection{Atomic mass unit and energy conversion}
One unified atomic mass unit is one twelfth of the mass of a free, neutral carbon-12 atom at rest in its ground state:
\[
1~\mathrm u\simeq1.6605391\times10^{-27}~\mathrm{kg},\qquad
1~\mathrm u\,c^2\simeq931.494~\mathrm{MeV}.
\]
Also $c=299\,792\,458~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ exactly and $1~\mathrm{eV}=1.602176634\times10^{-19}~\mathrm J$ exactly. Therefore
\[
\frac{(1.6605391\times10^{-27})(2.99792458\times10^8)^2}
 {1.602176634\times10^{-13}}\simeq931.494~\mathrm{MeV}.
\]
The denominator converts joules to MeV. Equivalently, $1~\mathrm u\simeq931.494~\mathrm{MeV}/c^2$. Do not equate mass with energy without the $c^2$ conversion. The speed of light is of order $10^{8}$, not $10^{-8}$, in metres per second.

The complete free-particle energy relation is $E^2=p^2c^2+m^2c^4$. At rest, $p=0$ and $E=mc^2$. Carbon-12 is a reference convention; it is not selected because carbon exists only as isolated atoms. Chemical bonding and nuclear stability are separate concepts.

\subsection{Mass defect and binding energy}
For a nucleus containing $Z$ protons and $N_n$ neutrons,
\[
\Delta m=Zm_p+N_nm_n-M_{\rm nucleus},\qquad B=\Delta m c^2.
\]
$B$ is the energy required to separate the nucleus into free nucleons, or released when the bound system forms. Mass defect has mass units; binding energy has energy units. They are related, not numerically interchangeable quantities.

If using tabulated neutral \emph{atomic} masses, the common teaching approximation is
\[
\boxed{B\simeq[Zm(^1\mathrm H)+N_nm_n-M_{\rm atom}]c^2.}
\]
Hydrogen atomic masses account for the $Z$ electrons. Do not combine a bare proton mass with a neutral atomic mass without correcting the electrons. More precisely, if $B_e$ denotes total positive electronic binding energy,
\[
B_{\rm nuclear}=[Zm(^1\mathrm H)+N_nm_n-M_{\rm atom}]c^2+ZB_e(\mathrm H)-B_e(X).
\]
The electron correction is small for light nuclei but matters in precise heavy-nucleus calculations. The exercises below explicitly use the usual approximation.

\subsection{Worked helium-4 calculation}
\extra
Using $m(^1\mathrm H)=1.00782503223~\mathrm u$, $m_n\simeq1.008664916~\mathrm u$, and $M(^4\mathrm{He})=4.00260325413~\mathrm u$,
\begin{align*}
\Delta m&=2(1.00782503223)+2(1.008664916)-4.00260325413\\
&\simeq0.0303766423~\mathrm u,\\
B&\simeq0.0303766423\times931.494\simeq28.2957~\mathrm{MeV},\\
B/A&\simeq7.0739~\mathrm{MeV\ per\ nucleon}.
\end{align*}

\subsection{Binding energy per nucleon and reaction energy}
$B/A$ helps compare average binding, and broadly rises toward the iron/nickel region before decreasing for heavier nuclei. Fusion of suitable light nuclei and fission of suitable heavy nuclei can release energy. Total $B$, $B/A$, and a particular reaction's $Q$ are different quantities.
\[
Q=\left(\sum m_{\rm initial}-\sum m_{\rm final}\right)c^2.
\]
For an isolated spontaneous decay, positive available $Q$ is required. Rest mass itself need not be conserved: total energy, including rest energy and kinetic energy, is conserved. Mass-number balance checks nucleons, not rest mass in kilograms.

\section{Activity and the exponential decay law}
\source{L3, approximately 1:00--36:00 and 41:00--45:00; L4, 3:00--6:00.}
\subsection{Random nuclei, predictable populations}
Radioactive decay is stochastic: one cannot predict the exact decay time of a particular nucleus. For a population of a single nuclear state with constant decay constant $\lambda$, the probability of decay in a small interval $dt$, conditional on survival, is approximately $\lambda dt$.

For the expected number $N(t)$ of surviving nuclei, with no production or removal other than decay,
\[
\frac{dN}{dt}=-\lambda N,\qquad \boxed{A(t)=\lambda N(t)=-\frac{dN}{dt}}.
\]
Activity is nonnegative. The minus sign compensates for the decrease in parent population. If a compartment also gains or loses nuclei biologically, $-dN/dt$ is \emph{not} purely nuclear activity; the nuclear-decay rate remains $\lambda_{\rm phys}N$.

\subsection{Derivation with an explicit reference time}
Separate variables and integrate:
\[
\int_{N_0}^{N(t)}\frac{dN'}{N'}=-\lambda\int_{t_0}^{t}dt',\qquad
\ln\frac{N(t)}{N_0}=-\lambda(t-t_0).
\]
Exponentiate both sides:
\[
\boxed{N(t)=N_0e^{-\lambda(t-t_0)}},\qquad
\boxed{A(t)=A_0e^{-\lambda(t-t_0)}}.
\]
\clarify{One \emph{applies} the exponential function to undo a logarithm; one does not multiply by $e$. Likewise, taking $\ln$ is not multiplication by $\ln$. $N_0$ is a number of nuclei and $A_0=\lambda N_0$ is activity.}
The exponent must be dimensionless. If $\lambda$ is in $\mathrm h^{-1}$, time must be in hours. For $A=\lambda N$ expressed in Bq, use $\lambda$ in $\mathrm s^{-1}$.

\subsection{Physical half-life}
At $t-t_0=T_{1/2}$, $A/A_0=1/2$. Thus
\[
\ln(1/2)=-\lambda T_{1/2},\qquad
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{\ln2}{T_{1/2}}},\qquad
\boxed{T_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{\lambda}}.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\boxed{\frac{A(t)}{A_0}=\frac{N(t)}{N_0}=2^{-(t-t_0)/T_{1/2}}}.
\]
A shorter half-life means a larger decay constant, not necessarily a larger activity unless the number of radioactive nuclei is also specified.

The continuous expected curve approaches zero asymptotically. A finite collection of discrete nuclei can eventually have no parent nuclei left; this does not contradict the exponential expectation. A detector can register zero counts during an interval even when the source is still radioactive. Background and detection limits further separate measurement from true activity.

\subsection{Units and useful conversions}
\[
1~\mathrm{Bq}=1~\text{nuclear transformation per second}=1~\mathrm{s^{-1}},
\]
\[
1~\mathrm{Bq}=60~\mathrm{dpm},\qquad1~\mathrm{Ci}=3.7\times10^{10}~\mathrm{Bq},
\]
\[
1~\mathrm{mCi}=37~\mathrm{MBq},\qquad1~\mathrm{\mu Ci}=37~\mathrm{kBq}.
\]
Becquerel and hertz share dimensions of inverse time but describe different phenomena. Counts per second (cps) and disintegrations per second (dps) are not generally equal. The curie historically relates to radium-226 activity; the modern conversion above is exact.

\subsection{Worked activity and scheduling calculations}
\extra
\textbf{1. Activity from nuclei.} For a teaching Tc-99m half-life of 6.00 h and $N=10^{15}$,
\[
T_{1/2}=21600~\mathrm s,\quad\lambda=3.2090\times10^{-5}~\mathrm{s^{-1}},
\quad A=3.2090\times10^{10}~\mathrm{Bq}=32.09~\mathrm{GBq}.
\]
This large sample activity is an arithmetic example, not a suggested patient activity.

\textbf{2. Forward decay.} A separated sample is 800 MBq at 08:00, with 6.00 h half-life. At 14:00 it is 400 MBq; at 02:00 the following day, 18 h have elapsed and it is $800/2^3=100$ MBq.

\textbf{3. Back-correction.} To have 300 MBq after 3 h for the same half-life, the earlier activity must be
\[
A_0=A(t)e^{\lambda\Delta t}=300\,2^{3/6}=424.26~\mathrm{MBq}.
\]
Earlier activity is larger. Neglecting handling losses is an explicit assumption.

\textbf{4. Solve for time.} The time to fall from $A_0$ to a lower $A$ is
\[
\Delta t=\frac{\ln(A_0/A)}{\lambda}=T_{1/2}\frac{\ln(A_0/A)}{\ln2}.
\]
Falling from 400 to 50 MBq with a 6 h half-life takes $6\log_2 8=18$ h.

\textbf{5. Infer half-life.} If a decay-only source falls from 200 to 125 MBq in 4 h,
\[
\lambda=\frac{\ln(200/125)}{4}=0.11750~\mathrm h^{-1},\quad T_{1/2}=5.899~\mathrm h.
\]
Measured count rates can substitute only when background is removed and geometry, efficiency and other response factors are unchanged.

\subsection{Limits of the constant-decay assumption}
Each nuclide and nuclear state has its own decay properties. Ordinary temperature and chemical changes generally do not materially alter the half-lives used in routine nuclear medicine calculations. Electron-dependent modes, especially electron capture, can be affected by electron occupancy; highly ionized systems require special treatment. A mixture does not usually follow one exponential, and daughter activity can increase through parent feeding.

\section{Mean life, specific activity and administered activity}
\source{L4, approximately 6:00--13:00.}
\subsection{Mean life is not an average of isotope half-lives}
For a single exponential lifetime distribution, measured from the reference time,
\[
S(t)=e^{-\lambda t},\qquad p(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t},\quad t\geq0,
\]
where $S$ is survival probability and $p$ is the normalized decay-time density. The $\lambda$ factor is necessary: $\int_0^\infty p(t)dt=1$.
\[
\boxed{\tau=\int_0^\infty t\lambda e^{-\lambda t}dt=\frac1\lambda
=\frac{T_{1/2}}{\ln2}\simeq1.4427T_{1/2}}.
\]
At $t=\tau$, the expected surviving fraction is $1/e\simeq36.79\%$. At $T_{1/2}$ it is 50\%. For the 6.00 h teaching half-life, $\tau=8.656$ h. Mean life is useful for any exponential radionuclide, not only long-lived ones.

For independent isotopes with initial activities $A_{i0}$,
\[

The apparent logarithmic slope changes as their proportions change. If asking for the mean remaining lifetime of a randomly selected radioactive nucleus, initial \emph{number fractions} $f_i$ give $\sum_i f_i/\lambda_i$; this still does not make the mixture single-exponential. \[
 a_{s,\rm pure}=\frac{\lambda N_A}{M}.
\]
The units of $M$ must match the desired mass denominator. Added stable carrier decreases activity per mass of the combined material. A high specific or molar activity may reduce the chemical mass administered for a given activity; it does not by itself specify patient absorbed dose.

Optimization must preserve diagnostic performance; ethnicity alone is not an adequate activity-selection rule. Adult and paediatric protocols also differ. See the EANM reference in Section~\ref{sec:refs}.

\extra
\textbf{Hypothetical arithmetic only.} If an exercise specifies $k=4~\mathrm{MBq/kg}$ and a 60 kg patient, then $A_{\rm target}=240$ MBq. If a solution is 120 MBq/mL \emph{at the administration reference time}, the ideal volume is

Actual dispensing additionally uses measured activity, timing, residual activity, validated geometry and the authorized protocol. Pre- and post-administration measurements must be decay-corrected to the same reference time before subtraction.

\subsection{Activity is not absorbed dose}
Activity is measured in Bq; absorbed dose is energy deposited per mass, in gray ($1~\mathrm{Gy}=1~\mathrm{J/kg}$). Equal activities of different radionuclides need not give equal organ doses. Emission energies and yields, spatial distribution, residence time and tissue mass all matter. A dose calibrator principally measures \emph{activity}; it does not directly measure a patient's absorbed dose.

\section{Radioactive decay processes}

\subsection{Conservation and a compact comparison}
Check charge, nucleon number, total energy and momentum in each complete equation; angular momentum must also be conserved. Ordinary beta and capture equations include a neutrino or antineutrino to complete energy, momentum and lepton accounting. Omitting them may abbreviate a chart label, but it does not give the full physical reaction.

Neutron and proton emission were named but not developed in detail in L4. Nuclear de-excitation can follow an earlier alpha or beta decay, so the categories are not mutually exclusive descriptions of an entire decay scheme.

\subsection{Alpha decay}
\[
\nucl{A}{Z}{X}\longrightarrow\nucl{A-4}{Z-2}{Y}+\nucl{4}{2}{He},
\qquad\nucl{238}{92}{U}\longrightarrow\nucl{234}{90}{Th}+\nucl{4}{2}{He}.
\]
An alpha particle is a helium-4 \emph{nucleus}, containing two protons and two neutrons with charge $+2e$, not initially a neutral helium atom. The daughter is a different element. Alpha emission is common in heavy radioactive nuclei and involves tunneling through the Coulomb barrier.

\extra
For a specified daughter level, alpha energy is discrete and the daughter recoils. In the nonrelativistic two-body approximation,
\[
Q=T_\alpha+T_D,\qquad T_\alpha\simeq Q\frac{M_D}{M_D+m_\alpha}.
\]
Several daughter levels produce several alpha lines. Excited daughters can subsequently de-excite. Alpha particles deposit energy densely over short ranges, making localization crucial in targeted therapy. Low penetration through intact skin does not imply low hazard after internal uptake.

\subsection{Beta-minus decay}
\[
n\longrightarrow p+e^-+\bar\nu_e,
\qquad\nucl{A}{Z}{X}\longrightarrow\nucl{A}{Z+1}{Y}+e^-+\bar\nu_e.
\]
The electron is created in the weak transformation; it is not a pre-existing electron stored in the nucleus. Mass number is unchanged because one neutron becomes one proton. The electron's nuclear-equation labels are $\nucl{0}{-1}{e}$: zero nucleons, charge $-e$, but \emph{nonzero rest mass}.

The lecture's example is
\[
\nucl{14}{6}{C}\longrightarrow\nucl{14}{7}{N}+e^-+\bar\nu_e.
\]
\extra
Electron kinetic energy is continuous because energy is shared with the antineutrino and recoil. A branch has an endpoint energy; this is not its mean beta energy. Using neutral atomic masses, approximately
\[
Q_{\beta^-}=[M(X)-M(Y)]c^2;
\]
subtract daughter excitation energy for an excited-state branch. Nuclear medicine beta emitters include I-131, Y-90 and Lu-177; photon emission depends on the full daughter scheme, not the beta-minus label alone.

\subsection{Beta-plus decay and annihilation}
For an energetically allowed transformation inside a nucleus,
\[
p\longrightarrow n+e^++\nu_e,
\qquad\nucl{A}{Z}{X}\longrightarrow\nucl{A}{Z-1}{Y}+e^++\nu_e.
\]
The free isolated proton does not spontaneously undergo this reaction: the nuclear mass balance is essential. The lecture uses $\nucl{10}{6}{C}\to\nucl{10}{5}{B}+e^++\nu_e$. A medically important example is $\nucl{18}{9}{F}\to\nucl{18}{8}{O}+e^++\nu_e$.

\extra
With neutral atomic masses,
\[
Q_{\beta^+}=[M(X)-M(Y)-2m_e]c^2.
\]
The parent--daughter atomic mass-energy difference must exceed $2m_ec^2\simeq1.022$ MeV for an allowed ground-state branch. Excitation adds to the requirement. The positron has a continuous initial kinetic-energy spectrum.

After slowing in matter, a positron usually annihilates with an electron:
\[
e^++e^-\longrightarrow\gamma+\gamma.
\]
For two-photon annihilation approximately at rest, each photon has about 511 keV and the directions are approximately opposite. PET detects these photons in coincidence. It does not directly detect a 511 keV photon emitted by the F-18 nucleus. Positron range and non-collinearity limit spatial resolution. Annihilation in flight and three-photon channels mean that ``always exactly two photons at exactly $180^\circ$'' is too absolute.

\subsection{Electron capture and atomic relaxation}
\[
p+e^-\longrightarrow n+\nu_e,
\qquad\nucl{A}{Z}{X}+e^-\longrightarrow\nucl{A}{Z-1}{Y}+\nu_e.
\]
Capture generally involves an occupied bound orbital with wavefunction overlap at the nucleus, commonly the K shell; other shells also contribute. It is a \emph{weak} transformation, not simply electrostatic attraction switching off nuclear forces. Like beta plus, it decreases $Z$ by one and increases $N_n$ by one at fixed $A$.

The lecture gives carbon-11 capture to boron-11. This is a possible minor branch; carbon-11 is primarily a positron emitter. Iodine-123 is a clearer medically relevant capture example:
\[
\nucl{123}{53}{I}+e^-\longrightarrow\nucl{123}{52}{Te}^{*}+\nu_e.
\]
The daughter can subsequently emit nuclear photons or conversion electrons. Capture also leaves an atomic vacancy. Filling a vacancy releases electronic transition energy through characteristic X-rays or nonradiative electron emission, potentially followed by a cascade.

\extra
The total atomic mass release is approximately $[M(X)-M(Y)]c^2$. Energy available to the neutrino in a specified channel is reduced by nuclear excitation, atomic vacancy energy and recoil. Capture has no positron-creation threshold and can occur when beta plus is forbidden, if the chosen final state is accessible. Neutrino energy is approximately discrete for a specified daughter level and captured shell.

\subsection{Gamma emission and metastable nuclei}
\[
\nucl{A}{Z}{X}^{*}\longrightarrow\nucl{A}{Z}{X}+\gamma.
\]
The nucleus loses excitation without changing proton or neutron number. Photon energy is approximately the level-energy difference, with a small recoil correction. A ground-state daughter need not be stable against later decay.

The important example is $^{99m}$Tc de-excitation to $^{99}$Tc, with a prominent photon near 140.5 keV. The daughter is \emph{Tc-99}, not Tc-99m, and remains long-lived and radioactive. Likewise, the familiar 662 keV line associated with Cs-137 arises from de-excitation of Ba-137m populated after Cs-137 beta decay.

\clarify{Gamma rays are distinguished from characteristic X-rays by their nuclear rather than electronic origin, not by a universal energy cutoff. Their energy ranges overlap. Annihilation radiation is another distinct origin of photons.}

\subsection{Internal conversion and Auger electrons}
\extra
Internal conversion transfers nuclear transition energy directly to an orbital electron, which is ejected:
\[
T_{\rm IC}\simeq E_{\rm transition}-B_{\rm shell}-E_{\rm recoil}.
\]
The nucleus keeps the same $A$ and $Z$. There is no real emitted gamma photon that must first be reabsorbed. Conversion competes with gamma emission and produces electron lines determined by the transition and shell. The vacancy can subsequently generate X-rays or Auger electrons. The conversion coefficient is $\alpha_{\rm IC}=N_{\rm conversion}/N_\gamma$ for the specified transition.

An Auger process is \emph{atomic} relaxation: energy from filling one vacancy ejects another electron. It is a nonradiative competing relaxation route, not necessarily an X-ray emitted and then trapped in the atom. In a simple shell-energy description,
\[
T_{\rm Auger}\simeq B_i-B_j-B_k,
\]
with more accurate calculations accounting for changed electron configurations. Conversion electrons originate from nuclear transition energy; Auger electrons originate from atomic rearrangement; beta electrons originate in a weak nuclear transformation.

\subsection{Spontaneous and neutron-induced fission}
Spontaneous fission requires no incoming projectile:
\[
\nucl{A}{Z}{X}\longrightarrow
\nucl{A_1}{Z_1}{Y}+\nucl{A_2}{Z_2}{W}+k\nucl{1}{0}{n},
\]
with $A=A_1+A_2+k$ and $Z=Z_1+Z_2$ for this simplified channel. Fragments, neutron multiplicity and emitted radiation vary. Cf-252 has a spontaneous-fission branch, although its dominant decay is alpha emission.

The lecture's uranium example is instead \textbf{neutron-induced fission}:
\[
\nucl{235}{92}{U}+\nucl{1}{0}{n}\longrightarrow\nucl{236}{92}{U}^{*}
\longrightarrow\nucl{141}{56}{Ba}+\nucl{92}{36}{Kr}+3\nucl{1}{0}{n}.
\]
This is one illustrative product channel, not the unique outcome. Charge is $92=56+36$ and nucleon number is $236=141+92+3$. The mass-92, $Z=36$ fragment is \emph{krypton}, not plutonium. Additional energy appears in fragment kinetic energy and radiation.

A moderator primarily slows neutrons; control rods primarily absorb neutrons to control reactivity. The emitted fission neutrons are initially fast, not automatically thermal. A chain reaction is not the same concept as a sequential radioactive decay chain.

\subsection{Weak reactions are not interchangeable reverse arrows}
\clarify{Ordinary beta-minus decay does not require an incoming neutrino. The process $\nu_e+n\to p+e^-$ is neutrino capture, a separate reaction. Positron capture and reverse weak reactions have their own incoming particles and energy requirements. Do not reverse a partial equation with missing neutrinos and call it the same spontaneous decay.}

\subsection{Physical, biological and effective half-life}
Physical half-life describes nuclear transformation. Biological half-life describes removal from a defined compartment. With independent single-exponential clearance and decay and no continuing uptake,
\[
\frac{dN}{dt}=-(\lambda_p+\lambda_b)N,\quad
\lambda_{\rm eff}=\lambda_p+\lambda_b,
\]
\[
\boxed{\frac1{T_{\rm eff}}=\frac1{T_p}+\frac1{T_b}},\qquad
\boxed{T_{\rm eff}=\frac{T_pT_b}{T_p+T_b}}.
\]
For finite positive $T_p,T_b$, $T_{\rm eff}$ is shorter than either. If $T_p=6$ h and $T_b=12$ h, $T_{\rm eff}=4$ h. Removal from an organ does not mean the radionuclide has decayed; it may move elsewhere. Uptake, recirculation and multiple compartments invalidate a one-exponential description.

\subsection{Time-integrated activity}
For $A(t)=A_0e^{-\lambda t}$,
\[
\widetilde A(0,t)=\int_0^t A(t')dt'=\frac{A_0}{\lambda}(1-e^{-\lambda t}),
\qquad\widetilde A(0,\infty)=\frac{A_0}{\lambda}=A_0\tau.
\]
With activity in Bq and time in seconds, integrated activity counts expected nuclear transformations. In a clearance compartment use the \emph{effective} decay of compartmental activity, yielding $A_0/\lambda_{\rm eff}$. Absorbed-dose calculation additionally requires emitted energy and the fraction deposited in each target, not merely integrated activity.

\subsection{Parent--daughter growth}
For a parent $P$ feeding a daughter $D$ with fraction $b$,
\[
\frac{dN_P}{dt}=-\lambda_PN_P,\qquad
\frac{dN_D}{dt}=b\lambda_PN_P-\lambda_DN_D.
\]
If $N_D(0)=0$ and $\lambda_D\ne\lambda_P$,
\[
N_D(t)=\frac{b\lambda_PN_P(0)}{\lambda_D-\lambda_P}
\left(e^{-\lambda_Pt}-e^{-\lambda_Dt}\right).
\]
Multiplying by $\lambda_D$ gives daughter activity. It can initially rise while parent activity falls. For a much longer-lived parent, secular equilibrium approaches $A_D\simeq bA_P$ after several daughter half-lives. When the parent is longer-lived but not overwhelmingly so, transient equilibrium may arise; the asymptotic ratio is $b\lambda_D/(\lambda_D-\lambda_P)$.

In a Mo-99/Tc-99m generator, Mo-99 (about 66 h half-life) feeds Tc-99m (about 6 h). Chemical elution separates technetium from molybdenum. Parent remaining in the generator produces more daughter. With ideal complete daughter removal, the daughter activity maximum occurs at
\[
t_{\max}=\frac{\ln(\lambda_D/\lambda_P)}{\lambda_D-\lambda_P}\simeq22.8~\mathrm h
\]
using 66 h and 6 h. Elution yield, breakthrough and radiochemical quality require actual quality-control measurements.

\subsection{Production routes}
Reactors produce radionuclides through neutron activation or fission-product routes; cyclotrons use accelerated charged particles in nuclear reactions; generators exploit parent--daughter separation. A production route must be checked for the specific nuclide. It is not sound to assume I-123, I-124 and I-131 are all inevitably co-produced in one reactor extraction. Radionuclidic purity concerns other radionuclides; radiochemical purity concerns chemical forms; chemical purity concerns nonradioactive contaminants.

\section{Supplement: from emissions to an image}\label{sec:imaging}
\extra

\subsection{Branching fractions and emission yields}
If competing channels have partial decay constants $\lambda_i$, then
\[
\lambda_{\rm total}=\sum_i\lambda_i,\qquad b_i=\lambda_i/\lambda_{\rm total},
\qquad A_i=b_i A_{\rm total}.
\]
The parent has one overall disappearance half-life, $\ln2/\lambda_{\rm total}$. A partial half-life is $T_{1/2}/b_i$. A branch fraction is not automatically the yield of a particular photon line: cascades can emit multiple photons and internal conversion can compete with photon emission.

\subsection{Activity, photon yield and count rate}
If $y$ is photons in the chosen emission line per transformation and $\epsilon$ is the total relevant detection efficiency,
\[
R_{\rm net}\simeq A\,y\,\epsilon.
\]
For 20 MBq, $y=0.80$ and $\epsilon=0.001$, the ideal photon emission rate is $16\times10^6$ photons/s and detected rate is 16000 cps. Activity, photon rate and count rate differ. Background, attenuation, scatter, dead time and the event-selection rules must be accounted for in a real measurement.

\subsection{Interaction and attenuation basics}
Photoelectric absorption transfers a photon's energy to a bound electron and leaves a vacancy. Compton scattering transfers part of the energy to an electron and redirects the photon. For a monoenergetic narrow beam through uniform material,
\[
I(x)=I_0e^{-\mu x}.
\]
$\mu$ is an attenuation coefficient in inverse length. The half-value layer is $\ln2/\mu$, which has length units; physical radioactive half-life has time units. Scatter entering a broad-beam detector complicates this simple attenuation model. Attenuation correction is essential to reliable quantitative emission imaging.

\subsection{Planar imaging, SPECT and PET}
A gamma camera uses a collimator to select photon directions and detector signals to estimate interaction positions and energies. Better geometric collimator resolution usually trades against sensitivity. Planar imaging superimposes activity through depth. SPECT acquires projections around the subject and reconstructs a tomographic activity distribution. Filtered backprojection and iterative methods are reconstruction approaches, not simply fixed properties of particular manufacturers.

PET uses coincidence lines of response between detected annihilation photons. True coincidences arise from the same annihilation; scattered coincidences include deflected photons; random coincidences combine unrelated events. PET/CT and SPECT/CT combine functional and anatomical information, with CT commonly supporting attenuation correction. Motion or misregistration can create artifacts.

For PET time of flight,
\[
\Delta x=\frac{c\Delta t}{2},
\]
where the sign depends on which detector's time is subtracted. Timing narrows probable location along a line; it does not eliminate the need for image reconstruction.

\subsection{Statistics and quantification}
For ideal independent counting with $C$ expected counts, Poisson standard deviation is approximately $\sqrt C$, so relative uncertainty is about $1/\sqrt C$. Four times the counts halve this component of relative noise. Background subtraction adds variance from both measurements. Dead time and correction/reconstruction methods can change the simple statistical model.

A body-weight-normalized SUV compares regional activity concentration with net administered activity per body mass, with consistent units and decay reference time:
\[
\mathrm{SUV}_{bw}=\frac{C_{\rm tissue}}{A_{\rm net}/m_{\rm body}}.
\]
Using Bq/g in numerator and denominator gives a dimensionless ratio. If imaging concentration is in Bq/mL, an approximately unit-density convention is commonly implicit. SUVs depend on uptake interval, glucose state, calibration, reconstruction, partial-volume effects and other conditions. They are not intrinsically diagnosis-specific.

\subsection{Diagnostic and therapeutic roles}
The same nuclear physics supports different clinical goals. Imaging favors emissions that escape tissue and can be detected with useful spatial and energy information. Therapy seeks energy deposition in the intended target while limiting irradiation elsewhere. A radionuclide can emit both therapeutic particles and useful imaging photons.

\subsection{QA/QC and radiation protection}
QA is the wider system ensuring reliable work; QC includes measurements that check performance. Examples include activity-meter constancy and geometry response, camera uniformity and energy response, SPECT centre-of-rotation alignment, and PET normalization and calibration checks. Frequencies and acceptance limits must follow the equipment, protocol and applicable guidance rather than a generic list.

Patient medical exposures are governed by justification and optimization; occupational/public dose limits are not patient diagnostic dose caps. Diagnostic reference levels help identify protocols needing review and are not individual patient limits. Time, distance and appropriate shielding reduce exposure; contamination monitoring addresses spread of radioactive material. These principles do not justify lowering activity below what the clinical task requires.

\section{Supplement: less common decay variants}
\extra
This section retains broader study coverage beyond the recordings without treating rare processes as routine imaging mechanisms.
\subsection{Particle emission and delayed emission}
Neutron emission gives $(A-1,Z)$; proton emission gives $(A-1,Z-1)$. A neutron-unbound state can emit a neutron without a Coulomb barrier; a proton-emitting state encounters a Coulomb barrier. Neutron-rich nuclei do not all emit neutrons: ordinary beta-minus decay is common.

Beta decay can first populate an excited daughter above a particle threshold, followed by particle emission:
\[
\nucl{A}{Z}{X}\xrightarrow{\beta^-}\nucl{A}{Z+1}{Y}^{*}
\longrightarrow\nucl{A-1}{Z+1}{Y}+n.
\]
The first step also emits $e^-+\bar\nu_e$. The delay reflects the parent weak decay. Two-proton and two-neutron emission may be direct/correlated or sequential, depending on accessible states. Cluster emission ejects a bound cluster heavier than an alpha, leaving $(A-a,Z-z)$ for an emitted $\nucl{a}{z}{C}$. These require nuclide-specific evaluated data.

\subsection{Other de-excitation channels}
An energetic nuclear transition can create an electron--positron pair internally when sufficient energy is available (above approximately 1.022 MeV, with recoil accounted for). This is distinct from beta plus: $A$ and $Z$ remain unchanged. It is also distinct from an already propagating photon producing a pair in matter. Rare two-photon nuclear de-excitation is another electromagnetic route.

\subsection{Double-weak and electron-occupancy effects}
Two-neutrino double-beta-minus decay has the form
\[
\nucl{A}{Z}{X}\longrightarrow\nucl{A}{Z+2}{Y}+2e^-+2\bar\nu_e.
\]
It is a second-order weak process rather than two ordinary decays through a populated intermediate state. Double electron capture changes $Z$ by $-2$ and consumes two electrons. Positron/capture combinations have their own mass thresholds and final-state requirements. These are extremely slow processes, not routine nuclear medicine production or imaging mechanisms.

A proposed neutrinoless double-beta mode would omit the antineutrinos and violate lepton number; it must be kept distinct from established two-neutrino modes. No experimental discovery claim is made here. In bound-state beta-minus decay, the newly produced electron occupies an available bound orbital, particularly relevant to highly ionized atoms. Radiative weak-decay variants include an additional photon. These qualifications explain why the simplest neutral-atom decay model is not universal.

\item \textbf{Partial half-life interval.} A 6 h source starts at 600 MBq. What remains after 9 h?\\
$A=600\,2^{-9/6}=212.13$ MBq. Non-integer numbers of half-lives are valid.

\item \textbf{Mean life.} A source has $\lambda=0.20~\mathrm h^{-1}$. Find mean life and half-life.\\
$\tau=5.00$ h and $T_{1/2}=0.693147/0.20=3.466$ h.

\item \textbf{Mixture.} Two independent components each start at 100 MBq and have half-lives 2 h and 6 h. Find total activity after 6 h.\\
$A=100/2^3+100/2=12.5+50=62.5$ MBq. Using an arithmetic mean half-life of 4 h would give a different, incorrect result.

\item \textbf{Integrated activity.} A single decay-only source starts at 100 MBq with a 6 h half-life. Find transformations during the first 6 h.\\
$\widetilde A=(100\times10^6)/(3.2090\times10^{-5})\times(1-1/2)\simeq1.558\times10^{12}$ transformations. Convert hours to seconds before combining with Bq.

\item \textbf{Net administered activity.} Pre-administration syringe activity is 250 MBq at 10:00. Residual is 10 MBq at 10:10. Using a 110 min half-life, calculate net activity referred to 10:00.\\
Residual back-corrected to 10:00 is $10\,2^{10/110}=10.650$ MBq; net is $239.350$ MBq. Both values need the same reference time.

\item \textbf{Effective half-life.} $T_p=8$ h and $T_b=24$ h.\\
$T_{\rm eff}=8\times24/(8+24)=6$ h. In the stated model compartmental activity falls to one quarter after 12 h.

\item \textbf{Alpha daughter.} $\nucl{210}{84}{Po}$ emits an alpha.\\
The daughter has $A=206$, $Z=82$: $\nucl{206}{82}{Pb}$. Include $\nucl{4}{2}{He}$ on the product side.

\item \textbf{Beta and capture.} Compare $^{14}$C beta minus with $^{18}$F beta plus.\\
C-14 gives N-14, an electron and an antineutrino. F-18 gives O-18, a positron and a neutrino. Neither process changes mass number.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
\item \textbf{Threshold test.} A hypothetical neutral-atom mass difference is 0.80 MeV/$c^2$. Is ground-state beta plus possible?\\
No: $Q_{\beta^+}=0.80-1.022=-0.222$ MeV. Electron capture may be possible if its nuclear and atomic final state is accessible; it must be checked separately.

\item \textbf{Conversion electron.} A 200 keV nuclear transition converts an electron bound by 30 keV.\\
Its kinetic energy is approximately 170 keV, neglecting recoil. This is not a continuous beta spectrum.

\item \textbf{Count precision.} Compare 10000 counts with 40000 counts.\\
Ideal relative Poisson uncertainties are 1\% and 0.5\%. Doubling counts alone improves relative uncertainty by $\sqrt2$, not a factor of two.

\item \textbf{Chart direction.} On horizontal $N_n$, vertical $Z$, what decay moves one square left and one up?\\
Beta minus: $(\Delta N_n,\Delta Z)=(-1,+1)$. Recheck axes before using directional mnemonics.

\item \textbf{Why is 511 keV not the beta-plus endpoint?}\\
It is approximately the energy of each photon from at-rest two-photon annihilation. Initial positron kinetic energies are distributed continuously according to the beta-decay branch.

\item \textbf{Does a ground-state daughter mean no further activity?}\\
No. Tc-99 is the lower nuclear state after Tc-99m de-excitation, yet is itself radioactive. ``Ground state'' and ``stable nuclide'' describe different properties.

\item \textbf{Can all nuclei in a small sample have decayed?}\\
Yes. The nonzero exponential curve is the expected population, not a guarantee that at least one parent survives in every finite realization.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Activity versus dose:} Bq counts transformations per second; Gy measures deposited joules per kilogram.
\item \textbf{Mass number versus atomic number:} $A$ counts nucleons; $Z$ identifies the element.
\item \textbf{Half-life versus mean life:} 50\% surviving at $T_{1/2}$; about 36.8\% at $\tau$.
\item \textbf{Primary versus secondary radiation:} beta plus produces a positron; annihilation produces the PET photons. EC or conversion can be followed by X-rays/Auger electrons.
\item \textbf{Reference times:} decay-correct activity and residual measurements before comparing or subtracting them.
\item \textbf{Precision:} check units, use isotope masses, retain guard digits, state approximation and round only the result.
